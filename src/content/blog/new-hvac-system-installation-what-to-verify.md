A new HVAC system installation replaces the heating and cooling equipment in a home, and in most cases the ductwork, electrical connections, and refrigerant lines that serve it. It is worth considering when your system is past roughly 12 to 15 years old, needs a repair costing a significant share of replacement, or can no longer keep the home comfortable. The single most important factor is not the brand you buy. It is whether the contractor performs a load calculation and verifies airflow after the install, because an oversized or poorly ducted system will underperform regardless of its efficiency rating. In California, most equipment changeouts also require a permit and independent third-party field verification, which is a useful way to confirm a contractor is doing the job properly.

This guide walks through what a real installation includes, when replacement beats repair, and the specific checks worth making before you sign a contract.

Replacement usually makes sense in four situations.

<tr><th scope="row">Comfort problems that persist after maintenance and duct repairs</th><td>If rooms still will not hold temperature, the system may be undersized, oversized, or badly matched to the duct system.</td></tr>

If the system is under ten years old and the failure is a capacitor, contactor, motor, or control board, repair is almost always the better financial decision. A contractor who jumps straight to replacement without documenting a failure deserves a second opinion.

## When Is the Best Time to Schedule Installation?

Spring and fall. Installations booked in the shoulder seasons give you time to compare bids, arrange financing, and schedule inspection without living through a heat wave with no cooling.

Emergency replacements in July compress every decision. You end up choosing from whatever equipment is in stock, and permit and inspection timelines still apply. If your system is near end of life, start the conversation before the season it will fail in.

## How Is System Size Determined?

Correctly sized equipment comes from a load calculation, not from matching the tonnage of the old unit. A Manual J load calculation accounts for square footage, insulation levels, window area and orientation, ceiling height, air leakage, and local design temperatures.

Oversizing is the most common error, and it causes real problems. An oversized air conditioner cools quickly, shuts off before it removes humidity, and short cycles, which wears the compressor and produces uneven temperatures.

A good contractor will walk the property, check duct condition, measure return air capacity, and confirm electrical capacity before quoting equipment. ## The Refrigerant Change Every Buyer Should Understand

This affects anyone buying equipment right now. Under the EPA's Technology Transitions rule, implemented through the American Innovation and Manufacturing Act, new residential air conditioners and heat pumps can no longer be manufactured or imported using refrigerants above a 700 global warming potential threshold. That moved the industry away from R-410A toward lower GWP A2L refrigerants such as R-454B and R-32.

Two practical points follow. First, existing R-410A systems remain legal to operate and service. Second, equipment manufactured before the cutoff can still be installed under the EPA's current rules, so you may be offered either type. Ask which refrigerant a quoted system uses, and ask how parts and refrigerant availability will look over the equipment's life. Current details are published on the [EPA's Technology Transitions Program page](https://www.epa.gov/climate-hfcs-reduction/technology-transitions).

A2L equipment also has its own installation requirements, including leak detection and specific line set practices. Confirm your installer is trained on it.

## Permits and Verification in California

Most HVAC equipment changeouts in California require a mechanical permit from the local building department, and many trigger independent field verification under the state's energy code, administered by the California Energy Commission. Typical verified measures include duct leakage, refrigerant charge, and airflow.

Treat this as a quality check rather than red tape. Independent verification means someone other than the installing crew confirms the system was charged correctly and the ducts are not leaking conditioned air into the attic. If a contractor offers to skip the permit to save money, that is a serious warning sign. It can complicate a future home sale, void manufacturer coverage, and leave you with no recourse.

## Common Mistakes Homeowners Make

- Choosing equipment by brand before anyone has evaluated the duct system
- Replacing only the outdoor unit and leaving a mismatched indoor coil, which undermines the rated efficiency
- Buying the highest efficiency tier while leaving leaky ducts untouched
- Accepting a quote with no model numbers, no scope of work, and no permit line item
- Skipping the airflow and charge measurements at commissioning
- Ignoring humidity control. In some homes, [HVAC humidifier installation](/services/indoor-air-quality-solutions/air-humidifiers/) or better filtration solves comfort complaints that new equipment alone will not

## How to Compare Contractor Bids

Ask every bidder the same questions, and compare the answers rather than the bottom line.

- Will you perform a Manual J load calculation, and can I see it?
- What are the exact model numbers for the outdoor unit, indoor coil, and furnace or air handler?
- Which refrigerant does this system use?
- Is the permit included, and who schedules the required field verification?
- What duct work is included, and what happens if you find problems once the old unit is out?
- What airflow and charge readings will you document at completion?
- What does the labor warranty cover, and for how long, separate from the manufacturer's parts warranty?
- Are your technicians EPA certified, and what is your contractor license number?

In California, HVAC contractors hold a C-20 license, which can be verified through the [Contractors State License Board](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/). HiLo Heating & Air operates under California license #1155955.

## Key Takeaway

Installation quality determines how a system performs far more than the badge on the equipment. Insist on a load calculation, a written scope with model numbers, a pulled permit, and documented commissioning readings. Have your ducts evaluated before you finalize equipment, since distribution problems will follow a new system into your home. Ask which refrigerant the quoted equipment uses and what that means for future service. And if your current system is aging but still running, start the process in a shoulder season, when you can compare bids calmly instead of choosing under pressure.
